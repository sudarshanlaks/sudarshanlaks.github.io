Inventory health

Where is stock tied up, and is the safety stock buffer bigger than it needs to be? An FMCG case study with synthetic data.

Tools
Python (pandas), Excel, interactive dashboard built with Claude
Data
40 SKUs, monthly stock and demand, 2024 to 2026. Synthetic, fictional brands.

Problem statement

A fictional FMCG company holds stock so that shelves never run empty. Stock also ties up cash, and half of it is a safety stock buffer that is only meant for emergencies. Finance asks whether that buffer is the right size, or whether cash is sitting idle.

The questions to answer:

  1. How much stock do we hold, and how many days of demand does it cover?
  2. How much of it is safety stock, and has that buffer ever been used?
  3. Which products hold more stock than they need?

Our approach

  1. Load the data

    Read inventory_supply_monthly.csv (1,800 rows: one per SKU and month, April 2023 to December 2026) and the product master products.csv, which adds unit cost and ABC class. Each row holds demand, month-end stock and the safety stock level.

  2. Clean and check

    Checked for duplicate rows, missing values, negative stock and SKUs missing from the product master: none found. The only lost sales are 31 rows in April and May 2023, when stock was still being built up, so the analysis starts in January 2024. The three projected months, October to December 2026, are kept only for a forward look. That leaves 1,320 actual SKU-months, September 2026 being the latest.

  3. Create the KPIs

    Calculated for every SKU and month, in Python:

    KPIHow it is calculated
    Inventory valueClosing units x unit cost
    Days of coverClosing units / monthly demand x 30
    Safety stock shareSafety stock value / inventory value
    Excess stockUnits above safety stock plus one month of demand
    Unused bufferThe lowest stock above safety stock a SKU ever had, x unit cost

  4. Test the buffer

    Counted every SKU-month where stock fell below safety stock, and found each SKU's lowest stock level as a multiple of its safety stock. A value of 1.0x would mean the buffer was used.

  5. Validate in Excel

    Rebuilt the results in an Excel workbook with live formulas on the same data (8,177 formulas, no errors). The Excel results match the Python results exactly.

  6. Build the dashboard

    One page with four KPI tiles, stock against safety stock over time, stock by category, the lowest stock level of every SKU and the SKUs with the most unused buffer. Month, category and ABC class filters recalculate every number.

What the data shows

  • Stock is worth EUR 2.90M, and 47% of it (EUR 1.36M) is safety stock.
  • No SKU fell below its safety stock in 33 months. The closest any SKU came was 1.13 times its safety stock, and the typical SKU never went below 1.8 times.
  • The unused buffer adds up to EUR 0.89M, mostly in Home Care and Personal Care.
  • 19 SKUs hold more than safety stock plus one month of demand, worth EUR 0.10M. 13 of them are C-class, which hold 64 days of cover against 46 for B and 55 for A.
  • No SKU is projected below safety stock in October to December 2026.

Recommendation

Lower safety stock in steps, starting with Home Care and Personal Care. Releasing half of the unused buffer would free about EUR 0.44M. Then slow replenishment for the 13 C-class SKUs in excess.

Limitations

The unused buffer looks backwards over 33 months and does not allow for future demand swings or supplier delays, so the buffer should be reduced in steps and reviewed after each one. Excess is defined as more than safety stock plus one month of demand; a different rule would give a different number.
